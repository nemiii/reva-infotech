The Real Cost of Running Procurement, Inventory, and Maintenance on Spreadsheets

Spreadsheets feel free. They rarely are — once you count what they're actually costing you.

FORGEAOS TEAM — PUBLISHED AUGUST 2026 — 8 MIN READ

Nobody sets out to run their operation on spreadsheets. It happens one workaround at a time: a stock count that needed tracking, a supplier list that needed sharing, a maintenance schedule that needed a home. Eighteen months later, procurement, inventory, and maintenance are each running on their own tangle of files — and everyone insists it's "working fine."

It is, technically. It's also costing you more than it looks like on the surface. Here's where that cost actually hides.

Where the cost hides

01

Double entry, twice a day, forever

Someone receives a delivery, logs it in the warehouse sheet, then re-types the same numbers into the procurement sheet so purchasing knows what arrived. That's not a one-time cost — it's a permanent tax on every transaction, paid by whoever has the patience to keep both sheets honest.

02

The "final" file that isn't

"Stock_FINAL_v3_reallyfinal.xlsx" is a joke because it's true. The moment more than one person edits inventory, you're one saved-over cell away from a decision made on numbers that were already wrong when someone looked at them.

03

Decisions made on numbers that are already stale

A spreadsheet is a snapshot, not a feed. The stock count someone's looking at right now was accurate whenever it was last updated — an hour ago, a shift ago, a day ago. Every purchasing and production decision made in between is a decision made on old information, and nobody can tell you exactly how old.

04

No real record of who changed what, or why

When a supplier disputes an order, or finance asks why a number doesn't reconcile, "check the spreadsheet history" isn't a real answer. Most sheets have no audit trail at all — just whatever the current cell says, with no memory of what it said yesterday or who changed it.

05

One person becomes a single point of failure

Every spreadsheet system eventually has one person who built the macros, owns the master file, and is the only one who really understands how it all fits together. When that person is out sick, on leave, or leaves the company, the system doesn't just get harder to use — it gets harder to trust.

06

It works for one site. It breaks at two.

A spreadsheet system that limps along for a single warehouse usually falls over once you add a second site. Now there are two "final" files, two versions of the truth, and someone's part-time job is reconciling them by hand.

A rough way to price it

You don't need exact numbers to know this is costing you — a rough estimate is usually enough to make the case for changing it. Two questions get you most of the way there.

The visible cost: how many hours a week does someone spend reconciling, re-entering, or double-checking data across sheets? Multiply that by their hourly cost, then multiply by 52. That number alone tends to be bigger than people expect.

The invisible cost: what did the last bad decision actually cost you — a stockout that halted a line, a duplicate order, a missed maintenance window? That number is usually the bigger one, and it's the one spreadsheets are worst at preventing, because nobody notices a stale number until after it's caused a problem.

Signs it's time to stop

None of these mean your team is doing anything wrong — they're just the usual signs a spreadsheet system has been outgrown:

  • More than one person maintains "the real" spreadsheet
  • A stock-out or duplicate order has happened because two sheets disagreed
  • Onboarding a new hire means teaching them which of six files is currently correct
  • You've added a second site, or you're about to
  • Nobody can answer "what changed and who changed it" with real confidence

What replaces it

The fix isn't a better spreadsheet. A shared drive with permissions and a naming convention just delays the same problems by a few months. The fix is one system that procurement, inventory, and maintenance all read from and write to at the same time — so there's only ever one current number, not six people's copies of it.
